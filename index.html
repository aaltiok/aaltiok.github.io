I am a Ph.D. candidate in Peace Studies and Political Science at the University of Notre Dame. As a comparative political scientist, I study how digitalization is reshaping youth political behavior and youth–state relations in Africa and the Middle East. My research combines original surveys, field experiments, and long-term fieldwork in Kenya and Türkiye, examining how young people interact with government-sponsored programs and how they act politically on social media.

Dissertation

Digital Hustlers: Youth Political Agency in Kenya

Young people are commonly flattened into reductive binaries, viewed either as threats to democratic stability due to their disengagement from conventional politics, or romanticized as drivers of democratic renewal through direct action against corruption and authoritarianism. Both portrayals rest on the assumption that youth harbor deep cynicism toward public institutions. My dissertation shows that most young people neither passively submit nor simply revolt. Under conditions of everyday economic precarity, they develop political tactics to navigate, negotiate, and contest political power.

In Kenya, the government invests in digital infrastructure and skills programs intended to connect young people to the online gig economy in a labor market unable to absorb a rapidly growing youth population. These programs shift responsibility for securing livelihoods onto young people themselves. Youth, in turn, appropriate them on their own terms, treating government-sponsored digitalization as a channel for accessing and extracting public resources while withholding or selectively extending political trust. I argue that digitalization has become a means through which states manage youth politics without resolving the structural precarity that produces prolonged transitions into adulthood, or “waithood.” Taking Türkiye as a shadow case, I examine whether this tactical engagement persists in an authoritarian setting where government-sponsored digitalization is embedded in militaristic and techno-nationalist development strategies.

I am developing the dissertation into a book manuscript that theorizes “digital hustling” as a broader form of youth political agency emerging at the intersection of economic precarity and digitalization.

The project draws on ongoing fieldwork in Kenya (2024 to 2026) across Western, Coastal, and Central regions, pairing broad empirical scale with contextual depth. Working with local digital skills trainers, who opened access to remote rural vocational ICT hubs, I administered an original survey (N=1,072) that gathered rich qualitative responses through audio prompts, alongside 60 semi-structured interviews and 55 focus group discussions. To examine digital youth political behavior, I conducted an in-person smartphone field experiment (N=320) uncovering how youth tactically engage with political deepfakes, pairing behavioral social media data and discernment judgments with 48 of these focus groups.

Teaching

My teaching spans comparative politics and international relations. I offer courses on political behavior, the politics of digital transformation, youth politics, Middle East and African politics, international organizations, and peace and conflict studies, together with research methods, including field methods, survey design, and experimental design in low-resource settings.

At the University of Notre Dame, I have taught as a teaching assistant for Middle East Politics and Introduction to Peace Studies, leading lectures on youth politics and counterterrorism. I regularly guest lecture on youth politics at universities in the United States and Europe, and for over ten years I have mentored young people from the Global South through collaborative research that treats youth as active partners.

I pair analytical research papers with policy briefs and position papers so that students learn to move between scholarly, policy, and public audiences without sacrificing analytical rigor. I also bring advanced students into my ongoing research on youth politics and digitalization, which can form the basis for independent projects and senior theses.
