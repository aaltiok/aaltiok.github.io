Ali Altiok

Ph.D. Candidate, Peace Studies and Political Science · University of Notre Dame

I study what makes young people trust, and mistrust, their governments in digitalizing states.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Peace Studies and Political Science at the University of Notre Dame. My research focuses on youth political behavior, specifically examining what drives young people's trust and mistrust in government, how they interact with state digital skills programs, and how they act politically on social media.

My dissertation relies on extensive original fieldwork in Kenya to examine youth political behavior in two settings: government-sponsored ICT hubs and social media. To evaluate institutional trust, I pair observational data from government digital hubs and 35 key-informant interviews with an original 1,072-respondent survey that integrates quantitative metrics with qualitative open-ended responses. To address digital youth politics specifically, I pair a smartphone field experiment on political AI deepfakes involving 320 participants with 48 focus group discussions on online engagement.

My scholarship maintains a direct connection to policy and practice. I bring extensive experience in policy-focused research, particularly concerning the UN Security Council's Youth, Peace, and Security Agenda, alongside expertise in UN multilateral counter-terrorism frameworks.

Dissertation

The Pragmatic Logic of Youth Political Trust in a Digitalizing Kenya

Young people are commonly flattened into reductive binaries, viewed either as threats to democratic stability due to their disengagement from conventional politics, or romanticized as drivers of democratic renewal through direct action against corruption and authoritarianism. Both portrayals rest on the assumption that youth harbor deep cynicism toward public institutions. My dissertation challenges this perspective by systematically analyzing what young Kenyans mean when they express trust or mistrust in their government and elected representatives. Uncovering a pragmatic political logic, I show that young people extend trust on material grounds, calibrating their support not as a simple transaction, but as an evaluation of what they judge elected representatives are realistically capable of delivering in their context. The dissertation examines three settings: how young people evaluate the state, how they encounter it through government digital programs, and how they contest it online. Across all three, political trust emerges as a grounded assessment of the possible rather than a final verdict on democracy itself.

Fieldwork

My dissertation draws on three rounds of original fieldwork in Kenya (2024 to 2026) across the Western (Kisumu, Siaya), Coastal (Mombasa, Kilifi, Kwale), and Central (Nairobi, Kiambu) regions. Rather than contracting a commercial survey firm, I built research partnerships with local youth trainers in each site. This community-embedded approach opened access to rural vocational centers accessible only by motorbike, capturing youth rarely represented in political behavior research. Across 29 institutions, I conducted 35 key-informant interviews, observational research, and an original 1,072-respondent survey that integrated open-ended audio responses directly into the quantitative instrument.

To examine digital politics at the edge of the digital divide, I designed an in-person smartphone experiment with 320 low-data participants to test responses to political AI deepfakes. Recruited in person at public Wi-Fi hubs, participants scrolled a simulated feed of authentic and manipulated media on their own handsets, recording real-time engagement before evaluating content authenticity. I paired the experiment with 48 focus group discussions to analyze how participants talk through online deception. By analyzing qualitative narratives alongside statistical metrics, the design ensures scale and contextual depth continuously inform each other.

Working Papers

Working paper

Beyond Trust Scores: How Kenyan Youth Interpret and Evaluate Government

Political trust is a complex evaluative process, but survey instruments compress it into a single score. Pairing open-ended audio responses with a 1,072-person survey, this paper recovers the reasoning behind the numbers: young Kenyans reporting identical trust scores are often making entirely different judgments, and their criteria are concrete and material rather than ideological. The gap between what trust scales measure and what respondents mean is systematic, not noise. What reads as generalized cynicism in the aggregate turns out to be a set of specific and defensible judgments about specific institutions.

Working paper

State Digitalization in Kenya: How Youth View Government-Sponsored ICT Hubs

Kenya's National Digitalization Master Plan promises an ICT hub in every administrative ward. Drawing on survey data, interviews, and observation across three regions, this paper treats the hubs as sites where young people meet the state directly. What the state actually supplies there is space and security, while infrastructure and training are underwritten by foreign firms and philanthropy.

Working paper

Laughing at Lies, Liking the Lie: Behavioral Signals of Deepfake Detection Among Kenyan Youth

Low institutional trust is widely assumed to leave young people credulous toward political manipulation online. A smartphone-based within-subjects experiment placed 320 young Kenyans in a simulated feed of authentic and manipulated political posts, recording what they did with each one before asking whether they believed it. Across 9,583 paired observations, engagement divides by communicative function rather than effort: behaviors that amplify credibility track lower accuracy, while ridicule and public correction track higher accuracy. Recognizing a manipulation does not end engagement but changes its form, and the form it most often takes is public evaluation rather than passive consumption.

Teaching

I am prepared to teach courses in comparative politics and political behavior, peace and conflict studies, African politics, international organizations, and research methods, including field methods, survey design, youth participatory research, and experimental design in low-resource settings.

At the University of Notre Dame, I have served as a teaching assistant for courses in Middle East Politics and Introduction to Peace Studies. I am also regularly invited to guest lecture on youth politics at universities in the United States and Europe. For over ten years, I have mentored young people from the Global South through collaborative research that treats youth as active partners.
